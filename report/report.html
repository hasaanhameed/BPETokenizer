Setup in brief

We collected 1.30 GB of cleaned Urdu text from four sources: VOA Urdu (news, scraped by us), Urdu Wikipedia, Urdu Wikisource (literature) and MADLAD-400 (web). The full details are in DATA_CARD.md. We trained a byte-level BPE tokenizer, written in plain Python, on the 1,276 MB train.txt to exactly 50,000 tokens: 256 bytes, then <|endoftext|> as ID 256, then 49,743 merges.

Training (fast trainer, full train.txt)307 s wall-clock on an Intel Core Ultra 7 155U (12 cores, 15.4 GB RAM), Python 3.14
Encoded corpustrain.bin 206.3M tokens, val.bin 2.0M tokens (flat uint16)
Fast vs reference trainerIdentical merges on a 1 MB sample of our corpus (from test.txt) for the first 5,000 merges. The fast trainer took 2.0 s and the reference took 1,578 s (about 26 minutes), roughly 790 times slower.

Pre-tokenization pattern. ?[\p{L}\p{M}\u200c]+| ?\p{N}{1,3}| ?[^\s\p{L}\p{M}\p{N}]+|\s+(?!\S)|\s+
The first branch treats letters, combining marks (\p{M}: zer, zabar, pesh, shadda) and ZWNJ as one word. So اُردُو stays a single piece instead of breaking at every diacritic, as it does with GPT-2's pattern. As in GPT-2, a leading space belongs to the word that follows it. Numbers are split into chunks of at most three digits, so the tokenizer can't spend merges memorizing long numbers. Every character matches some branch, which keeps encoding lossless. Normalization (Arabic → Urdu letters, all digits → ASCII, ZWNJ kept only between letters) happens before training and is not part of the tokenizer.

Rebuilding letters. Every Urdu letter is two bytes in UTF-8, so most early merges just put letters back together: 22 of the first 50 merges join two bytes into one letter, starting with ی, ا and ک. The order follows letter frequency. Rare letters come much later (ژ at merge 1,177), and so do diacritics (zer at 164, pesh at 225). Over the whole vocabulary, 325 merges rebuild a two-byte character.

Invalid UTF-8 tokens. Only 3 of the first 50 merges (1, 5 and 19) and 208 of all 49,743 (0.4%) are not valid UTF-8 on their own. Merge 1 is a space plus the byte d8, which is the first half of most common letters. Nearly every Urdu word starts with "space + d8/d9", so this pair is counted more often than any complete letter. These half-letters are stepping stones: merge 12 (␣ا) is built from merge 1, and merge 49 (ائ) from merge 19. The later invalid tokens are fragments of 3-byte characters such as curly quotes and dashes (e2 80), plus some Hindi and Bengali script that leaked in through Wikipedia.

First whole word. Merge 29 gives ␣کے ("of"), which appears 61,451 times in test.txt. It's followed soon after by ␣کی (32), ␣میں (38), ␣ہے (44) and ␣اور (47). These are the postpositions and helper verbs that hold every Urdu sentence together. By merge 5,000 the tokenizer is adding full content words. By the last merges it is adding rare names such as ␣چانڈیو (Chandio), which shows the vocabulary has started to fit corpus-specific words.

Measured in characters, the longest tokens are mostly English, and most of them aren't ordinary words. They are pieces of website names from Wikipedia's reference sections (githubusercontent, minorplanetcenter), repeated on thousands of pages, plus separator lines made of ۔ and *-. The longest real Urdu tokens are loanwords such as ␣انسائیکلوپیڈیا (encyclopedia) and ␣انٹرکانٹینینٹل (intercontinental). Each Urdu character costs two bytes, so the same merge budget covers fewer Urdu characters. The table shows where the corpus is still noisy: reference boilerplate in Wikipedia uses up some merges.

On ordinary Urdu our tokenizer is clearly the most efficient. It uses 1.29 tokens per word on test.txt. o200k_base needs 33% more tokens, and GPT-2 needs about 4.5 times as many, because its English-trained vocabulary has almost no Urdu merges and falls back to bytes. Our news and poetry samples are close to one token per word. This matters for Assignment 2: the same context window will hold several times more Urdu text.

The advantage disappears outside Urdu script. With English words mixed in, and especially in Roman Urdu, o200k_base wins, and for Roman Urdu even GPT-2 beats us. Our 50,000 tokens were spent almost entirely on Urdu, while the other two tokenizers were trained on huge amounts of Latin-script text. Diacritics and names cost us more than plain news, but we still use far fewer tokens than both other tokenizers there.

Q3. Three inputs our tokenizer handles badly

Below, each token is shown in its own box. Red boxes are byte fragments that are not valid UTF-8 on their own.

1. Roman Urdu: 2.64 tokens/word (the same sentence in Urdu script: 1.00)

Mujhe␣Urdu␣bolna␣bohat␣pasand␣hai␣lekin␣likhna␣mushkil␣lagta␣hai

"Mujhe Urdu bolna bohat pasand hai lekin likhna mushkil lagta hai" becomes 29 tokens. The same sentence in Urdu script becomes 11, one per word. Our cleaning kept only Arabic-script lines, so the tokenizer saw almost no Roman Urdu (a gap noted in the data card). The few Latin merges it learned come from English names and references in Wikipedia. So common Roman Urdu words such as bohat, hai and mushkil are split into 2–4 pieces. o200k_base does better here (1.82 tokens/word).

2. Text typed with Arabic letters and digits: 2.80 tokens/word (after normalization: 1.07)

پاd983ستان20 dbb1dbb4␣اگست20 dbb1dbb9dbb4dbb720 d983و␣آزاد20 d987وا…

This sentence, "پاكستان ۱۴ اگست ۱۹۴۷ كو آزاد هوا…", was typed with Arabic kaf ك, heh ه, yeh ي and Eastern-Arabic digits. That's common when someone uses an Arabic keyboard. These characters look the same as the Urdu ones, but they are different code points. Our normalizer maps all of them to Urdu letters and ASCII digits before training, so the tokenizer never saw them and learned no merges for them. Each one falls back to two raw bytes and breaks the word around it, so پاكستان becomes four tokens instead of one. The fix is to run normalize() before encode(), as our pipeline does. The tokenizer itself must stay lossless, so it doesn't normalize.

3. Emoji: 5.33 tokens/word (the same text without emoji: 1.00)

بہت␣خوب20 f0 9f 9882f0 9f9882f0 9f94a5f0 9f918df0 9f8fbd

"بہت خوب 😂😂🔥👍🏽" uses 2 tokens for the words and 14 for four emoji. Each emoji is 4 bytes in UTF-8, and a thumbs-up with a skin tone is two code points (8 bytes). Our corpus is edited news, encyclopedia and literature text, which contains almost no emoji. So only a few shared byte prefixes such as f0 9f became merges, and each emoji still costs 2–3 tokens of meaningless byte fragments. Nothing is lost, and decoding still gives back the exact text. But chat-style Urdu would be expensive to model. o200k_base, trained on web and chat data, uses 10 tokens for the same input.
